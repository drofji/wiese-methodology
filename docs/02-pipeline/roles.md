# Who Decides, Who Works

Friday night, a full restaurant. The head chef doesn't stand at every pan. She takes the orders, hands the work out, tastes what leaves the kitchen and sends a plate back when it isn't right. The cooks cook, someone fetches what's missing from the store room, and the guests only ever talk to one kitchen, not to six people shouting at once. Nobody in that kitchen wonders who decides, and that is exactly why the evening works.

Working with AI gets messy in the same place a kitchen does: when it isn't clear who decides and who does the work. This chapter sorts that out.

---

## Roles, not job titles

This methodology isn't about who is whose boss. Company charts belong to the company, and they change from one place to the next. What the methodology cares about is the person actually doing the work, and that person can wear different hats in a single afternoon: in the morning you write a task for someone else, in the afternoon you take on a task yourself.

So we talk about roles, never titles. The **author** decides what should be built and why, which makes the author the architect of the work. The **executor** does the work, and that can be a person just as well as an AI. The **reviewer** looks at the result before anyone relies on it. In a large project there is also an **owner** for each part, the one who keeps the rules for, say, the payment part, much like one person in a family is the one who knows when the car needs its check-up; in a small project the author simply does this too. An automatic check by the AI isn't a role at all, it's a step the work passes through.

A role is a job you do right now, not a name on your door.

---

## One session runs the show

As soon as you work with several AI sessions at once, a new role shows up: the **main session**. It's the head chef from the opening scene. You talk to it, and it is the only one that talks to you. It splits the work, starts helper sessions, gives each one its task, looks at what they bring back, and keeps the whole picture in one place. If the person is the architect, the main session is the operator that keeps the building site running.

Two things never leave the main session. The first is the conversation with you, because one voice is easier to follow than five, and because a question that is yours to answer must reach you, not get settled somewhere out of sight. The second is saving and publishing, so the commit and the push, and even those wait for your yes. Checking the helpers' work, on the other hand, can be handed to another helper, which gives a fresh pair of eyes and lets the main session move on in the meantime, as the chapter on [checklists](../03-quality-gates/automated-gates.md) explains. Decisions are never handed down: the main session makes them itself or brings them up to you.

Many hands are fine, as long as there is one head.

---

## A helper gets the whole note

When you leave your kids with a babysitter, you don't hand over your entire week of messages and hope they find the bedtime in there. You write one page with everything they need. A helper session gets the same treatment: one complete task that stands on its own, the kind described in [Flow Stages & Atomic Tasks](stages.md), not a copy of the long conversation it came from. A long conversation buries the important part in the middle, and a single clear task doesn't.

The way back is just as fixed. When a helper plans the work, it doesn't start the next helper itself; it brings the plan back to the main session. That sounds like a detour, but it's the point. The plan gets checked before anything is built on it, control stays in one place so nothing starts without the main session knowing, and if the plan holds a decision that belongs to you, there is still a moment to ask. Nobody in the kitchen starts a new dish because another cook thought it would be nice.

Every road out of the main session leads back to it.

## The right model for the job

You wouldn't hire a top architect to carry boxes up the stairs, and you wouldn't ask the friend who helps you move to design the new kitchen. AI models come in sizes too, and the methodology picks them the same way: the strongest model thinks things through, weighs options and looks for the cause of a problem; a quick, small one searches and looks things up; a middle one writes text and code. At the time of writing, one maker's line-up, for example, has a large, a middle and a small model under the names Opus, Sonnet and Haiku, and other makers offer the same kind of spread under their own names.

Using the strongest model for everything sounds safe, but it isn't the smart move. A small model finds a file just as well, only faster and cheaper, it leaves your subscription's limit for the work that really needs a heavy thinker, and often a model that fits the task simply does it better than one that overthinks it.

Fit the tool to the job, not the job to the biggest tool.

---

## Three things only you do

Some things stay with a person every single time, however good the AI gets. The first is working with real customer data, because that data was trusted to people, not to a model. The second is anything that leaves the circle of people working on the project, an e-mail to a client or a file sent outside, which stays a draft until a person has checked it. The third is the decision to publish, because a page other people have seen can't be taken back.

Everything else can be shared out, checked and handed around. These three are yours.
